Serviced Accommodation: The Occupancy Reality

11 July 2026 5 min read Property Kickstart

Ask a Serviced Accommodation investor how their property is performing and the answer you'll usually get is their peak week. August rates. The bank holiday weekend. The corporate booking that filled the whole month. The best number is always available — because it's memorable and because it feels like evidence of what the strategy can do. The number that's harder to get is the average month: what the property actually produces across a full year, after voids, after running costs, after the quieter months that never make it into the story.

That gap — between peak performance and average performance — is where most of the misunderstanding about Serviced Accommodation sits. It's not that the strategy doesn't work. In the right locations, with the right management, Serviced Accommodation can produce strong results. The conditions that make it work, however, are more specific than the headline nightly rates suggest — and understanding those conditions before committing to the strategy is what separates a well-executed investment from one that underperforms the model significantly.

The Gap Between Nightly Rate and Annual Average

Serviced Accommodation income works differently to long-term residential let income. Instead of a fixed monthly rent, income varies with occupancy: high in peak periods, lower in shoulder seasons, potentially very low in off-peak periods depending on location. The headline nightly rate — £120/night, £150/night, £200/night — is a peak or high-season rate. It is not the rate the property achieves 365 days a year.

The metric that actually matters for Serviced Accommodation investment analysis is annualised revenue: the total income the property generates across a full year, divided by 365 nights. This produces the effective daily rate — the average the property actually earns per night after accounting for voids, seasonal variation, and periods of lower demand. For most Serviced Accommodation properties, the effective daily rate is meaningfully lower than the headline nightly rate — sometimes by 30–50% depending on location and demand profile.

Understanding this distinction changes the investment maths entirely. A property achieving £150/night at 65% annual occupancy generates an effective daily rate of £97.50. At 50% occupancy, it's £75. The cost structure — mortgage payments, management fees, utilities, laundry, consumables, platform fees, insurance — doesn't vary proportionally with occupancy. Many Serviced Accommodation costs are fixed or semi-fixed regardless of how many nights the property is let. So the occupancy level isn't just a revenue variable: it's the variable that determines whether the investment makes money or loses it.

When the conditions are right, Serviced Accommodation can produce excellent results. When they are not, the additional complexity often outweighs the additional income.

Running Costs: What the Nightly Rate Has to Cover

The operating cost structure of a Serviced Accommodation property is substantially higher than a long-term residential let. Every turnover between guests involves cleaning, laundry, and restocking of consumables. Platform fees on Airbnb, Booking.com, or equivalent typically run between 15% and 20% of gross booking revenue. Utilities — electricity, gas, broadband — are paid by the owner, not the guest, and in a well-used property these costs are significant.

If using a professional management company, fees are typically 20–25% of revenue — higher than standard Buy to Let management rates and reflecting the more intensive operational requirement. If self-managing, those management costs are replaced by the investor's own time — which is real and should be accounted for honestly in the analysis.

The practical effect of the Serviced Accommodation cost structure is that the gross-to-net gap is wider than in a long-term residential let. A Buy to Let property might have a gross-to-net gap of 1.5–3% of property value per year. A Serviced Accommodation property's gap, expressed in the same terms, is often larger — even before accounting for the occupancy variable. Net yield modelling for Serviced Accommodation needs to be done on realistic occupancy assumptions, realistic running cost estimates, and a realistic nightly rate that reflects the full year rather than the peak.

Occupancy, Seasonality, and the Locations Where Serviced Accommodation Works

Seasonality is the variable that most materially affects whether a Serviced Accommodation investment performs as modelled. In a purely seasonal location — a coastal town or tourist destination with a clear peak season and a quiet off-season — occupancy can vary dramatically between months. A property that achieves 90% occupancy in July and August might achieve 20–30% in January and February. The annual average from those two extremes may look reasonable on paper; the cashflow reality involves several months where income falls short of fixed costs.

The Serviced Accommodation locations that produce the most consistent results are those with year-round demand drivers: major cities with strong business travel and leisure demand; locations adjacent to large employers, hospitals, or universities that generate consistent contractor and professional accommodation demand; and areas with events and attractions spread across the calendar rather than concentrated in a single season. These locations produce flatter demand curves — less spectacular peak periods, but stronger off-peak occupancy that keeps the annual average meaningful.

This is why evaluating a Serviced Accommodation deal requires location-specific demand data rather than generic yield calculations. The right questions are: what drives demand in this location in off-peak months? What is the competition — how many other Serviced Accommodation properties are operating in the same area, and how does that affect occupancy rates? Is there evidence of year-round corporate or professional demand, or is this location primarily leisure-driven?

There is also a planning dimension investors increasingly need to understand. Some councils across the UK have introduced Article 4 directions, while others require planning consent for certain forms of Serviced Accommodation use. A property that works under today's rules may require a different approach in future. Understanding the local planning position before you buy is becoming part of good due diligence.

For investors evaluating Serviced Accommodation who are uncertain whether a specific location and property type justify the operational complexity, it is worth comparing the full net yield picture against simpler strategies. HMO and standard Buy to Let have lower peak income potential, with more predictable cost structures and demand profiles — and for many investors, that predictability has real value. The right starting point is always understanding what you actually need the investment to do before deciding which strategy is best placed to do it.

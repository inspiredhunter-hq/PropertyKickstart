Development Opportunity: Are You Ready to Finish?

14 July 2026 5 min read Property Kickstart

The question most investors ask about a Development Opportunity is whether they have enough capital to acquire it. If the numbers say yes — purchase price covered, acquisition costs accounted for — they consider themselves ready. Acquisition, however, is only the beginning. The measure of readiness for a development project is not whether you can get into it. It's whether you can see it through to completion and a defined exit — with the capital, time, team, and clarity of purpose that requires.

Development projects test investors differently to income-generating strategies. There is no rent coming in while work is underway. There are costs — some predictable, some not — at every stage of the build. The timeline from acquisition to realising the outcome is longer than most investors model, and the variables that affect that timeline are more numerous. Investors who are genuinely ready for Development Opportunities have thought through all of this before they commit. Investors who discover it after committing often find the process more demanding than they anticipated.

The Capital Commitment Most Investors Underestimate

Development projects require capital in multiple tranches and at unpredictable intervals. The acquisition cost is the starting point; the build cost — which is the majority of the total capital commitment — comes after, and it rarely lands exactly as modelled. Construction costs fluctuate with materials prices and contractor availability. Scope changes happen. Surveys reveal unexpected issues. Planning requirements add costs that weren't in the original appraisal.

Professional development appraisals build in a contingency reserve — typically 10–15% of the total build cost — specifically to absorb the unexpected. This isn't pessimism: it's the industry standard because cost overruns on development projects are common enough that not budgeting for them is the riskier position. An investor who has exactly enough capital to fund the acquisition and the base build cost, with no contingency, is exposed to any of a long list of events that would require additional capital mid-project.

There is also the question of holding period and the costs it generates. During the build phase, the investor is typically paying finance costs — development finance, bridging, or the opportunity cost of deployed capital — without receiving any income from the asset. These are not one-time costs; they accrue over the full duration of the build, and a project that runs longer than anticipated produces higher ongoing costs than modelled. Investors who haven't stress-tested their capital position against a longer-than-planned build period are taking on risk they may not have priced in.

The investors who succeed with Development Opportunities are rarely the ones who simply had enough capital to start. They're usually the ones who understood the full commitment before they committed: capital, time, team, and exit.

The Time Commitment — and Why It's Consistently Underestimated

Development projects take longer than investors expect — almost universally. Planning delays, contractor scheduling, materials lead times, and weather all affect construction timelines. A project modelled at eight months frequently takes twelve or fourteen. A project involving any complexity — listed buildings, mixed-use elements, unusual planning conditions — can extend significantly beyond initial projections.

The time variable matters in two ways. First, it affects ongoing costs, as described above. Second, it affects the investor's ability to commit attention and oversight to the project. Development projects require active engagement from the investor — particularly around planning, contractor management, and key decision points during the build. Investors who have limited time available, or who have other significant commitments running concurrently, need to build that reality into their assessment of whether a particular project is manageable for them.

This isn't about discouraging busy investors from development — it's about being clear on what the project will require and whether you have the capacity to provide it. Some investors are able to delegate effectively to a project manager or development manager, which addresses the time constraint — and adds to the cost structure, which in turn requires a trusted and competent team already in place before the project begins.

Exit Clarity — the Variable That Determines the Whole Project

Every Development Opportunity has an exit: the point at which the investor realises the value created by the project. The two primary exits are sale (selling the completed development to a buyer) and refinance and hold (refinancing onto a long-term product and retaining the asset for income or further appreciation). Both are legitimate strategies, and they require different conditions — and being unclear on which exit you're targeting, or assuming market conditions will support your preferred exit when the time comes, introduces significant risk.

A sale exit requires a market that will absorb the completed product at a price consistent with the appraisal. Development appraisals are built on Gross Development Value — an estimate of what the completed units will sell for at the start of the project, based on current market conditions. By the time the project completes — potentially 12–24 months later — market conditions may have shifted. Investors who are relying on a specific GDV to make the numbers work need to have thought carefully about the conditions under which that value might not be achieved.

A refinance and hold exit requires a completed asset that can be financed on standard lending terms, at a value that allows the investor to extract a meaningful amount of capital and retain the asset on acceptable terms. This requires the completed development to have a rental yield and asset value that supports the refinance maths — which in turn requires the build cost to have been managed to a level that leaves enough value in the deal after finance costs.

The investors who approach Development Opportunities most successfully are those who are explicit about their exit before they start — who have modelled the project under both their primary exit and a realistic downside scenario, and who have enough flexibility in their capital position to handle a delay or partial shortfall in either. That level of preparation is what readiness actually means in this context.

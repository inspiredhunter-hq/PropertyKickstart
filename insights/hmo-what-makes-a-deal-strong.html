The number most investors lead with when evaluating HMO deals — bedroom count — is the least useful indicator of whether a deal will actually perform

9 July 2026 5 min read Property Kickstart

The first thing most investors ask when they see an HMO deal is how many bedrooms it has. The second is usually the gross yield. Both are proxies for the same question — how much rent does this produce? — and both sidestep the variables that actually determine whether the deal will perform. Bedroom count drives gross yield. But HMO deal performance is determined by something else entirely.

This matters because HMOs carry a more complex cost structure than single-let property. Licensing, compliance, utilities, furnishing obligations, higher management intensity — the gap between gross yield and net yield on an HMO is typically wider than on a Buy to Let, and the variables that drive it are less predictable. An investor who leads with bedroom count and stops at gross yield is building their analysis on the part of the deal that tells them the least.

Why bedroom count misleads — and what to look at instead

Bedroom count determines the theoretical rental ceiling of an HMO — the maximum rent the property could generate if every room were let, at the achievable market rate, with no voids. In practice, that ceiling is rarely reached and the gap between theory and reality is where most HMO underperformance lives.

A five-bedroom HMO in a weak rental location will carry higher voids than a four-bedroom HMO in a strong one. The five-bedroom property generates a higher gross yield figure because the inputs — bedroom count and theoretical rent — are larger. The actual net cash in the investor's account each month may be lower. Bedroom count, as a primary analysis tool, actively misleads by rewarding size over quality of location and demand.

Gross yield has the same problem. It calculates what the property earns before the costs that make HMO management genuinely different from single-let. HMO licensing fees, mandatory furnishing, higher utility costs (in bills-included tenancies), more frequent tenant turnover, and the compliance overhead of managing a multi-occupancy property all reduce what the investment actually puts in the investor's pocket. None of these costs appear in the gross yield figure.

The question that matters is not "how many rooms and what's the gross yield?" It is: "does this location, at this price, with this cost structure, produce a net return that justifies the capital and operational commitment?"

A strong HMO deal is not defined by bedroom count or gross yield. It's defined by whether the location, cost structure, and management capacity all stand up together — and all three have to stand up.

Location — the most underweighted variable in HMO analysis

Location does more work in HMO analysis than in almost any other residential strategy, because the HMO tenant demographic is specific and their location requirements are non-negotiable. HMO tenants — typically young professionals, postgraduates, or workers in specific industries — need proximity to employment or education. They need accessible transport. They need the amenities that support a shared-living lifestyle. A property that is 45 minutes from the nearest employment centre on public transport will struggle to let rooms consistently, regardless of how many rooms it has or what the gross yield looks like.

Strong HMO locations share a consistent set of characteristics: they sit within reasonable commuting distance of major employers or university campuses; they have good transport links; the local rental market has demonstrated demand for room-let rather than whole-property tenancies; and the supply of competing HMOs has not outpaced demand to the point where incentives or rent reductions are required to let rooms. These are the conditions under which void rates stay low and rooms let quickly when they become available.

This location analysis has a direct relationship to net yield. A property in a weak HMO location will carry higher void rates — rooms empty for longer between tenants — which reduces effective annual rent, which compresses net yield. A location analysis is, in part, a void rate analysis, and void rate is one of the most significant line items in the gap between gross and net return on an HMO.

Location, cost structure, and management capacity
The three variables that determine HMO deal strength. All three must stand up independently. A strong location with a broken cost structure is not a strong deal — and a manageable cost structure in a weak location will not compensate for persistent void.

Management — the operational requirement most investors underestimate

HMOs are operationally intensive in a way that single-let properties are not. This is not a reason to avoid them — the returns on a well-run HMO in a strong location can be significantly higher than on a comparable Buy to Let investment — but it is a requirement that has to be factored into the analysis before committing to a deal.

The management requirements of an HMO include: more frequent tenant movement (and therefore more frequent re-letting), more complex compliance obligations under the HMO licensing framework, utility management in bills-included tenancies, communal area maintenance, and a higher frequency of maintenance calls relative to a single-let property of similar size. All of these add cost, add time demand, or both.

The investor's choice of management approach shapes the economics of the deal materially. Self-managing an HMO significantly reduces management cost but adds an ongoing operational commitment that needs to suit the investor's life. Using a specialist HMO management agent reduces that commitment but adds a management fee — typically higher than on a single-let — which compresses net yield further. Neither approach is wrong; both have to be modelled honestly in the deal analysis rather than assumed away.

What fails consistently is the assumption that management is an afterthought — something to figure out after the deal is secured. Investors who buy HMOs on the basis of gross yield and bedroom count without a clear management plan often discover that the cost and operational reality of the investment is different from what they expected. The analysis that prevents this is the analysis done before the purchase, not after it.

A strong HMO deal is one where the location supports consistent tenant demand, the cost structure produces a defensible net yield after all running costs, and the management arrangement — self-managed or agent-managed — is one the investor has committed to and planned for. When all three stand up together, bedroom count is a consequence of the right deal, not a reason for it.
